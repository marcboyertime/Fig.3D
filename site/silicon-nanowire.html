How silicon breaks from within

Silicon can hold about ten times more lithium than the graphite in today’s battery anodes, but it swells as it charges, and it cracks. In this paper, researchers charged single silicon nanowires inside an electron microscope, watched them change, and built a computer model to explain why they break.

The experiment

A crystalline silicon wire about 200 nm across touches a lithium source. Lithium enters at one end, and a reaction front sweeps along the wire, turning silicon into a lithium–silicon alloy that takes up far more room. Near the source, the wire grew from 216 to 580 nm across.

The surprise

The wire does not swell evenly. Its round cross-section grows into a dumbbell, much wider along one crystal direction than another. Behind the front, cracks open along the wire, although nothing is pushing on it.

What Figure 5 shows

The central idea

Nothing pushes on it,
yet it tears itself

The wire carries no outside load. Stress comes from within: newly lithiated silicon wants to be larger, but it stays bonded to silicon that has not reacted yet, so neither can take its preferred shape.

As the crystalline core shrinks, that internal balance shifts. The surface indent that was squeezed shut is pulled open instead. The authors observe cracks running along the wire behind the advancing front, and their model explains why they start at the indent.

What the model is

An explanatory geometry, not a rerun of the authors’ simulation. Its length and viewpoint are fitted to the printed wire of Figure 5a; the final section matches their simulated widths (2.62 and 1.14 times the pristine diameter). Intermediate shapes, the progression and the two-colour lithium map are illustrative.

Stress arrows show sign only. Every stress value lives in the original panels; nothing here solves mechanics, kinetics or fracture.

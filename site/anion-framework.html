Paper companion · Wang, Richards, Ong et al. · Nature Materials 2015

Why some crystals let lithium fly

Why does lithium race through a few sulfide crystals but crawl through most others?

A solid electrolyte has to let lithium ions through almost as freely as a liquid does, and only a handful of crystals manage it. This paper found what the best of them share: their sulfur ions are arranged like the atoms of a body-centred cubic (bcc) metal. In that arrangement lithium can step straight from one small cavity to the next.

The paper

The authors mapped the sulfur in real fast conductors onto ideal lattices and found it close to bcc. Then they computed one lithium hop through bare bcc, fcc and hcp sulfur, with no other ions present, at 40 Å³ per sulfur, and again from 28.5 to 70.8 Å³.

What you’re looking at

Sulfur (yellow) and the gaps between it where lithium (green) can sit: tetrahedral T sites with four sulfur neighbours (green cages) and octahedral O sites with six (red). The lattices are exact geometry, and the bcc view is fitted to the paper’s Figure 2a. The energies are the paper’s; the lithium’s route between them is drawn.

How to use it

Start with Figure 2. Move a lithium ion through each lattice and watch its energy. Then squeeze and stretch the lattice, and finally strip a real crystal, LGPS, down to the bcc pattern hidden inside it.

The central idea

Every stop
the same

Lithium sits most happily in a tetrahedral gap, with four sulfur neighbours. In a bcc sulfur lattice every tetrahedral gap shares a face with four others, so lithium can travel through the whole crystal stepping only from one tetrahedral site to the next, each as good as the last. The highest point of each step is low: 0.15 eV at 40 Å³ per sulfur.

In the far more common fcc and hcp packings, tetrahedral sites do not connect into a network on their own. Lithium has to pass through an octahedral site, which costs more energy, and the barrier rises to about 0.4 eV. The paper puts that difference at about three orders of magnitude in room-temperature conductivity. The paper’s design rule follows: look for crystals whose anions sit close to bcc.

What the model is

The three lattices are ideal geometry at the paper’s volumes, with every tetrahedral and octahedral site and every shared face computed. The bcc viewpoint is fitted to the paper’s Figure 2a render, whose ten sulfur spheres it matches to 0.03 Å. The lithium moves on straight lines through the centre of each shared face. That follows the sites the paper names, but it is not the calculated path. LGPS is the published Kamaya structure; its sulfur matches a bcc lattice to 0.58 Å here, as in the paper’s Table S1.
